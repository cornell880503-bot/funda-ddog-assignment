## 1. What this project actually was

I ran a complete buy-side research question end to end — source selection, point-in-time panel construction, validation, a live call, and a monitoring surface. The output was a **negative result**: no alternative-data construction beat a correctly specified naive baseline.

A negative result is a weak research deliverable and a strong product one, because the interesting artefact is not the answer. It is the **list of things that almost produced a false positive** — fourteen of them, each logged with the decision, the alternative rejected, and what it would have cost.

That list is a product requirements document. Every entry is a place where a competent analyst, or an LLM agent, would have shipped a wrong answer with high confidence.

## 2. Six requirements, each earned by an incident in this project

| # | Requirement | The incident that motivates it | What it would have cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R1** | **Point-in-time vintage as a service.** Every value carries the date it became *public*, not the date it was filed or the date of the period. Features are a filter over vintages, never a judgement. | Used the 10-Q filing date as "the date the number became public". The market learns it from the 8-K, up to **18 days earlier** for Q4. Separately: `pct_change(4)` on a panel with gaps compared 2019Q4 to 2018Q3 and printed a fake **+122.5%** growth number. | Every Q4 backtest silently look-ahead biased; one fabricated growth print feeding every downstream model. |
| **R2** | **No signal without a registered control.** A signal is not a series, it is a *pair* — the series and an economically unrelated comparison built the same way. Unregistered = unshippable. | Datadog npm downloads grew **+112% YoY** in 2026Q2, apparently confirming a revenue acceleration. The control cohort grew **+184%**. Later, in text: a **lawyer's boilerplate disclaimer** "predicted" earnings surprises at **r = −0.81**, beating management's own words. | A confident bullish call on ecosystem-wide inflation. In the text case, a "significant" NLP signal from a legal template. |
| **R3** | **Provenance checks on signal composition.** Before a basket is admitted, verify its members are independent of each other and present across the whole window. | `dd-trace` v5 — **82.6% of installs** — carries `@opentelemetry/api` in its dependency closure, so the obvious control basket contained traffic generated by the numerator. Separately, three Datadog packages did not exist at sample start; including them added a permanent **0.31 log-point** jump. | A denominator contaminated by its own numerator, and a level shift indistinguishable from growth. |
| **R4** | **Integrity checks at ingestion, not in analysis.** Anomaly detection runs on the raw feed, cross-entity, before anything is derived. | The npm API returned registry-wide zeros on **12 of 3,512 days** — four of them inside the live quarter, including the most recent day. Removing 0.34% of observations flipped a headline correlation from *below* its placebo to *above* it. | A spuriously bearish live nowcast, and a published correlation that reverses on twelve days of data. |
| **R5** | **The evaluation harness is infrastructure, not the analyst's choice.** Baseline suite, matched placebo, multiple-comparison accounting and a power report all run automatically, and the analyst cannot opt out. | AR(1) alone is a weak opponent: a **bare time index** beats it. Against AR(1), 15 of 24 cells looked like wins; against a drift-aware baseline, **0 of 24**. And "0 of 24" is only meaningful alongside the power result — at n=13 a genuine 5% edge is detected **6%** of the time. | A shipped signal that was measuring trend, defended with a real p-value. Or the mirror error: a true small edge dismissed as nothing. |
| **R6** | **Coverage triage before modelling.** For each candidate source, record what share of the economic quantity it observes and whether history exists — before any analysis is run. | I analysed npm because it was easy to observe. Datadog's core billable unit ships through Docker Hub, which carries **10× the volume** and publishes only a lifetime counter with **no history**. One API call would have surfaced this on day one. | Six phases of rigorous work on roughly a tenth of the relevant surface. |

**The pattern across all six:** none of these are analytical mistakes in the sense of bad statistics. They are places where the *default behaviour* of a careful person — or of an LLM agent fetching the current version of a source — produces a confident wrong answer. That is precisely what infrastructure is for.

## 3. Why this is sharper for an LLM agent than for a human

Three of the six are *characteristic* agent failures, not generic ones:

- **R1 is the canonical one.** Ask an agent for "Datadog's Q2 revenue" and it fetches today's value from today's page. It has no concept of what was knowable at a decision date. The failure is silent, produces plausible numbers, and invalidates every backtest built on it.
- **R2 gets worse with unstructured data, not better.** §3.6 of the report is the cleanest demonstration I have: text drifts on many slow dimensions, and an extractor will happily quantify all of them. A legal disclaimer beat management's own language. An agent pipeline that adds LLM extraction *without* adding the control requirement increases false-positive rate rather than coverage.
- **R5 is where agents are structurally better than humans.** A human runs the baseline they think of. A harness runs all seven, plus the placebo, plus the permutation null, every time, at no marginal cost. This is the strongest argument for agentifying research: not that the agent is a better analyst, but that it is a tireless adversary against its own conclusions.

## 4. Build sequence, and why in this order

**V0 — the substrate (nothing is valid without it).**
Vintage service (R1) and coverage triage (R6). These are the two that invalidate *all* downstream work when absent, and both are cheap: vintage is a schema decision plus a CI test; triage is a checklist plus one API call per source. Ship these before any signal work, or every signal shipped before them has to be re-validated later.

**V1 — the adversary.**
Control registry (R2, R3) and the evaluation harness (R5). A signal cannot enter the registry without its control; the harness runs baselines, placebo, permutation null and power on every candidate and returns a verdict rather than a chart. Output is a standard card: *ratio vs strongest baseline, CI, control result, minimum detectable effect.*

**V2 — coverage expansion, under the same rules.**
Unstructured extraction (transcripts, filings, research) — but only after V1, because R2 is what makes it safe. Extraction quality is a second-order problem; control discipline is first-order.

**Deliberately not in the sequence:** more signals. This project's finding is that signal count is not the constraint. The constraint is the ability to tell a signal from a trend, and that is what V0 and V1 buy.

## 5. What changes for the analyst

| | Today | With this system |
|---|---|---|
| "Is this signal real?" | A week of ad-hoc work, and the answer depends on which baseline the analyst happened to pick | A verdict card in minutes, with the baseline suite fixed in advance |
| Ahead of earnings | Read the signal chart, form a view | Headline from the validated model; signals flag *when the model is likely to break* |
| A negative result | Usually unpublished, so the same idea is re-tested next year | Registered with its power bound, so "we could only have detected >10%" is durable knowledge |
| Onboarding a new ticker | Rebuild from scratch | Three parameters: CIK and revenue tag, signal basket with its control and placebo, guidance extractor |

The behavioural change that matters is the third row. Research organisations systematically lose negative results, so the same dead ends get re-explored. A registry that stores *what was tested, against what baseline, at what power* is a compounding asset in a way that a signal library is not.

## 6. How we would know the product works

Model metrics are not product metrics. Four I would instrument first:

1. **Placebo pass-through rate** — share of shipped signals whose matched control also beats the baseline. Target: near zero. This is the direct measure of false positives escaping, and it is only computable because controls are mandatory.
2. **Signal survival at re-validation** — share of shipped signals still beating their baseline two quarters later. Measures whether we are shipping trend-fitting. This project predicts a low number for anything validated without R5.
3. **Time from question to verdict** — the analyst-facing latency that determines whether the tool gets used at all.
4. **Override rate on divergence flags** — how often analysts dismiss a flag. Persistently high means the thresholds are miscalibrated; persistently zero means nobody is reading them.

Metric 1 is the one I would put in front of an institutional client, because it is the thing they cannot verify themselves and the thing that determines whether the output is trustworthy.

## 7. The honest limitation of this document

This is a spec grounded in one ticker, one analyst, two weeks. It has not been tested against a real research team's workflow, and the sequencing in §4 reflects what *this* project needed rather than what a client would rank first. The first thing I would want after joining is to run the R6 triage against the questions clients actually ask, because I would expect that to reorder §4 — and finding that out early is cheaper than being right about it now.
